# **MY FINDINGS:**


- The most repeated idea is the three ways to learn: **passive learning**, **knowing by first-hand experience** (learning enough to rediscover it yourself), and **grokking** (making the learned knowledge your own). It mainly emphasizes continuously trying to apply and implement what you learn, making mistakes, and learning from them. The more mistakes you make, the more you understand aspects that are not visible at first glance. **Be a producer rather than a consumer.**
- **Mastering a tool over mastering shortcuts** — it takes years, months, days...
- **Tools are force multipliers**, and in order to go 10x, you need to **build your own tools**. You master a tool by internalizing it and breaking it down to first principles — understanding why and where to use it. When you find a worthy problem, do not settle for a point solution; instead, aim to generalize it.
- **Thought process is important when learning.** Textbooks and most research papers tend to present chunks of technical theory rather than explaining how we arrived there. **Haruki Murakami** gives an example: a genius writer may produce work effortlessly, while an average writer struggles to write even a single paragraph. But when the genius loses inspiration, they may be stuck because they never developed a process to move forward.
- **10x is a frame of mind, a mentality.** If you aim for a moonshot and fail, you still land among the stars. Incremental improvements are not wrong, but you risk becoming obsolete as the world moves forward. **“Do only what you can do” — Dijkstra.** In the 80–20 principle, focus on the biggest hurdles, as most outcomes come from them.
- Prototype fast to fail.
- Write down ideas and represent them using math, as math reveals flaws in your solution more clearly (not just numbers, but mathematical modeling).
- Experiment relentlessly and figure things out.
- When it comes to **reading research papers**, the best approach is to **grok them, challenge their claims, and poke holes in them**, rather than accepting everything at face value.
- **Context matters** — the environment, setting, and motivation behind the problem you are solving are all important.
- **Learn through mistakes and iteration** — make assumptions, test them, correct them, and form new hypotheses. This is not the most efficient process, but it leads to deeper understanding. We cannot store infinite information, but we can decompose complexity and retain the ability to reconstruct knowledge. For example, understanding the meaning behind formulas allows you to recreate them instead of memorizing them. Continuously revisit concepts, vary conditions, and explore their behavior to build a strong intuition. You truly understand something when you can teach it well — through blogs, papers, or explanations. This process also helps you understand why things fail.
